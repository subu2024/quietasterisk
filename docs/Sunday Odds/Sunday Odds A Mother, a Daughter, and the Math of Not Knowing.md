# Sunday Odds: A Mother, a Daughter, and the Math of Not Knowing

Oct 11, 2026 · @Sam

## The premise

**Sunday Odds** is a recurring series of conversations between a hedge fund analyst and her newly graduated daughter, held at the same kitchen table every Sunday. Each article is one conversation about one money question, answered in the language of odds, not certainties.

The idea running through every episode: **nobody knows what happens next, so the skill is pricing what you don't know.** Revathi does this for a living with other people's billions. Anika has to do it with her first $3,900 a month. Same math, very different stakes, and neither of them is allowed to hide behind jargon or vibes.

Two things sit under the whole series. First, a **decision-making framework for uncertainty**: the Four Doors (what deserves your attention) and the Decision Ledger (an eight-step routine for choosing well). Second, **worked case files**, where the family applies the framework to a real company, starting with a full analysis of NVIDIA: what it sells, who competes with it, what it is worth, and at what prices they would buy, hold or sell.

**Who it's for:** people in their 20s and 30s who feel behind on money, and parents who want to talk about it without a lecture. It is also for anyone who has read "invest early, stay consistent" a hundred times and still doesn't know what to do on Monday.

**What makes it different from advice columns:**

- Every episode ends with a *Monday Move*: one action that takes under 30 minutes.
- Every concept gets a napkin calculation with real numbers, so the reader can redo it with their own.
- The two disagree on purpose. Revathi is not always right, and Anika's instincts are sometimes better than the textbook.
- Predictions are written down with probabilities and scored later. The series keeps its own track record, in public.

*Educational content, not personalized financial advice. Figures are illustrative unless sourced; readers should check current rates, prices, tax rules and limits.*

## The duo

### Revathi Krishnan, 53: the one who prices everything

Senior portfolio analyst at a multi-strategy hedge fund (fictional, unnamed). Twenty-six years in markets: equity research, then volatility and options, now risk-adjusted positioning across a book she describes only as "large enough to embarrass me if I'm sloppy." Raised Anika mostly alone after her divorce, which is where her instinct for buffers came from.

- **Voice:** dry, exact, short sentences. Teaches with kitchen and weather analogies because she cooks every Sunday while they talk. Says "call it" before any estimate and never says "guaranteed."
- **Signature lines:** "Say it in odds." · "What would have to be true?" · "Boring is a position." · "A budget is a forecast, not a prison."
- **Flaw:** over-hedges her own life. Anika has caught her keeping $40,000 in cash for a decade "just in case."
- **Private ritual:** the *Kill Journal*, a notebook of her worst calls and what she believed at the time. She reads one aloud when Anika gets too confident.

### Anika Krishnan, 23: the one who asks the right dumb question

Graduated in June 2026 with a degree in communications and a minor in statistics she mostly regrets. Now a product operations associate at a mid-size logistics startup, salary $62,000, $24,000 in student loans, shared two-bedroom apartment, and a best friend, Emma, who is always "about to make it" in crypto and says "just do it, you only live once."

- **Voice:** fast, funny, allergic to lectures. Talks in screenshots and group-chat references. Interrupts with "ok but what do I do Monday?"
- **Signature lines:** "Translate it to rent." · "Be so for real, what are the odds?" · "Okay, that actually makes sense, I hate it."
- **Strength:** reads how her generation actually behaves (subscriptions, gig income, job-hopping, finfluencers). Revathi's models sometimes miss this, and Anika knows it.
- **Flaw:** impatient. She wants the shortcut, then feels guilty when she takes it.
- **Private ritual:** her own *Ledger*, a running list of predictions with percentages, scored months later. In Season 2 it grows into the full Decision Ledger.

## Article format: the same bones every week

Every chapter runs 1,200 to 1,800 words, is published as one article per week, and follows the same seven beats, so readers know where to look and you can write fast. Each chapter is one "episode": one Sunday, one question.

| Beat | What it is | Length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Cold open | A 3-5 line scene from Anika's week that raises the question (a paycheck, a group chat, a job offer) | \~100 words |
| 2. The Question | Anika states what she actually wants to know, in her words | \~50 words |
| 3. Revathi's Frame | The core concept, explained through an analogy and framed as odds | \~300 words |
| 4. The Napkin | A calculation with real numbers the reader can redo. Assumptions listed first | \~250 words |
| 5. The Disagreement | Where they differ, and who has a point | \~200 words |
| 6. Monday Move | One action under 30 minutes, with exact steps | \~100 words |
| 7. The Ledger | Anika logs a prediction or decision with probabilities. Past entries get scored | \~100 words |

**Case file chapters** (the stock analysis arc) keep the same beats but swap the Napkin for a *Case Sheet*: a short table of sourced, dated figures, followed by a scenario calculation. Case files run over several consecutive Sundays so each chapter still has one question.

### Recurring devices that create continuity

- **The Ledger.** Anika's predictions, each with a probability ("I'm still at this job in 12 months: 70%"). Scored with a simple Brier score every few chapters. From Season 2 it becomes the Decision Ledger, an eight-step page she fills out for every real decision.
- **The Four Doors.** Do, Decide, Delegate, Delete: a way to sort what deserves attention by importance and urgency. The running warning: apps and feeds manufacture urgency to push Door 4 into Door 1.
- **The Asterisk.** Any certainty gets a small star and the question "what can't I see?" It closes every case file.
- **The Kill Journal.** One of Revathi's past mistakes appears whenever a chapter touches overconfidence. By the end of a season the reader has a small museum of how smart people lose money.
- **The Learning Portfolio.** A capped $500 account Anika runs as "tuition," opened in Season 3. Every real trade she makes in it becomes material for later chapters.
- **The Number.** Anika's running net worth, published at the end of each season, including the ugly negative months.
- **Emma.** Anika's best friend and the source of hot tips, and a standing test of how to say no to a friend without losing one.

### House rules for writing it

1. Every concept must be redoable with the reader's own numbers.
2. No prediction without a probability. No probability without a time frame.
3. Revathi is allowed to be wrong in public, at least once per season.
4. Say what is assumed (tax rate, return, interest rate) and tell readers to swap in current figures.
5. Case files analyze a named company as a worked example. Every company figure is pulled from a filing or another source, dated, and cited; no number from memory. The case ends with value ranges and "what would change my mind," never with an instruction to the reader to buy or sell.
6. Judge a decision by its process, not its outcome. Say so out loud every time a result is shown.

## Chapter 1: The First Paycheck Is a Forecast

*Sunday, October 11. Kitchen table. Onions are already browning.*

Anika slides into the chair with her phone held out like evidence. The screen shows her bank balance: $3,912.

**Anika:** It's real. A whole month of me, deposited. I feel rich.

**Revathi:** (not turning from the stove) You feel rich for roughly nine days. Then the rent clears.

**Anika:** Mom.

**Revathi:** I'm not trying to ruin it. I'm trying to forecast it.

### The Question

**Anika:** Okay, fine. I make $62,000, and I have no idea what to do with the money that actually shows up. Everyone online says "pay yourself first" and "invest early," but none of them say what to do *Monday*. So: what do I do with $3,912?

### Revathi's Frame: plan for the bad month, not the average one

**Revathi:** First, stop thinking of your paycheck as a number. It's a number *you control*. Your spending isn't. The paycheck is the same every two weeks. Your expenses are a range. Some months there's a flight home, some months a dentist, some months you buy a jacket you'll regret.

**Anika:** So?

**Revathi:** So what were your last three months of spending?

**Anika:** (scrolls) $2,900. $3,400. $3,100.

**Revathi:** Average is about $3,130. Most people budget to the average. Don't. You'll be over budget in roughly half your months, and every overrun feels like failure. Budget to the bad month. That's $3,400. Call it the 80th percentile: only about one month in five should beat it.

**Anika:** That seems pessimistic.

**Revathi:** It's accurate. A budget is a forecast, not a prison. Forecasts have error bars. Plan for the top of yours and the good months become free savings instead of a missed target.

**Anika:** Okay. That's... oddly less depressing than I expected.

### The Napkin

Revathi pulls a napkin from the holder and clicks a pen. *Assumptions are written first: they are illustrative, so swap in your own.*

- Take-home pay: **$3,912** a month (after tax and a 4% 401(k) contribution)
- Planned spending, bad-month version: **$3,400**
- What's left: **$3,912 − $3,400 = $512**, so round to **$500** a month into a buffer

**Revathi:** Now, why does the buffer exist? Don't say "because it's smart." Give me a number.

**Anika:** ...I can't.

**Revathi:** Then here's one. Say that in any given year there's about a coin-flip chance you hit a $1,000 surprise: car trouble, a medical bill, a trip you can't skip. Call it 50%.

|  | With a $1,000 buffer | Without one |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where the $1,000 comes from | Your savings | A credit card at \~24% APR |
| Cost if the surprise happens (carried one year) | \~$0 | \~$240 in interest |
| Expected cost (× 50% chance) | \~$0 | \~$120 |
| What the buffer gives up | Savings earns ~~4% (~~$40) instead of a possible stock return of ~~7% (~~$70), so about **$30** a year | n/a |

**Revathi:** So the buffer costs you about $30 a year in forgone return and saves you about $120 in expected interest. And that ignores the real risk: the year the surprise lands on the same month the market is down 20%, and you're forced to sell at the worst price.

**Anika:** So my first investment is a savings account. Wow. Very exciting. Very 2026.

**Revathi:** Boring is a position. You're long optionality: the right to not panic.

### The Disagreement

**Anika:** Okay, but Emma says a stock she's in is going to 5x by spring. If I put the first $500 there instead...

**Revathi:** Is it a lock, or is it a story that ends with the word "5x"?

**Anika:** Be so for real, what are the odds?

**Revathi:** No. You tell me the odds. What would you have to believe for it to 5x?

**Anika:** ...I don't know. That Emma's right?

**Revathi:** That's the most honest thing anyone's said about a stock this year. The money isn't forbidden. It's *sequenced*. Buffer first, match second, then you get a small, capped account to learn on. I'll even help you set it up. That's later.

**Anika:** And you? You've had $40,000 sitting in cash for ten years.

**Revathi:** (pause) That is a different chapter.

**Anika:** It's a *very* good chapter.

**Revathi:** I'm aware.

### Monday Move (25 minutes)

1. **Open a separate savings account** at a different bank from your checking. The small friction of having to log in elsewhere is the point. Choose a high-yield account and check the current rate rather than trusting last year's number.
2. **Automate $500 on the day after payday.** If your employer allows split direct deposit, send $500 straight to savings so you never see it.
3. **Name the account** something that makes you laugh and keeps you honest, like "Future Anika's Car Repair."
4. **Check your 401(k) match.** Find out if your employer matches contributions and up to what percentage. If you aren't contributing at least enough to get all of it, change that this week. A match is an immediate return on the money you put in, and nothing in the market guarantees that.

*First target:* $1,000. That covers most common surprises. Revathi's sizing for a full emergency fund comes in Chapter 2.

### The Ledger

Anika opens a notebook and writes at the top: **Predictions. Written before I know the answer.**

| # | Prediction | Probability | Date it resolves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | I have at least $1,000 in the buffer | 85% | Dec 31, 2026 |
| 2 | I am still at this job | 70% | Oct 11, 2027 |
| 3 | I break the "Emma's stock" rule and buy something impulsively | 60% | Dec 25, 2026 |

**Revathi:** (reading over her shoulder) Number three. You're low.

**Anika:** What would you put?

**Revathi:** Seventy-five. And write that down too. If I'm right, I get to say I told you so in December. If I'm wrong, it goes in the Kill Journal.

**Anika:** There's a Kill Journal?

**Revathi:** There's a Kill Journal. Eat your sambar.
