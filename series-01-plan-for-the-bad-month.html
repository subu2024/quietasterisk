Plan for the Bad Month

Anika's first full paycheck, and why a budget needs error bars.

budgetingaveragesemergency fundprobability

Sunday, September 27, 2026. Revathi's kitchen, Fairfax, Virginia.

On Saturday night, Anika's budgeting app greets her with a green ring and the words You're crushing it. The balance reads $5,200, her first full month of salary, landed in two neat checks. The app has never met her landlord, her student loans, or her best friend Emma, but it is very proud of her anyway.

She screenshots it and sends it to no one. The next afternoon she drives to her mother's house in Fairfax with the phone face up on the passenger seat, glowing.

Revathi is at the stove in her navy cardigan, a pencil in one hand, tipping mustard seeds into hot oil with the other. They pop like tiny applause.

RevathiDon't tell me. You have discovered money.

AnikaI've discovered my money. All $5,200 of it showed up. Look.

RevathiAnnie. Phone down. Taste this.

The Question

AnikaOkay, real question. $92,000 sounds enormous when I say it out loud. Then $5,200 lands and I realize I have no plan for it. Every video I watch says "pay yourself first" and then cuts to a sponsor. What do I actually do on Monday?

Revathi's Frame: plan for the bad month

RevathiStart with something dull. What did you spend the last three months?

Anika(scrolling) $4,700, $4,400, $4,100.

RevathiAnd what does your app say you can spend?

Anika"Safe to spend: $4,400 a month." That's the average. If I come in under it, there's confetti.

RevathiThen the app is built to be wrong in about half your months and to flatter you in the other half. An average describes a typical month. It says nothing about the bad one, and the bad one is the one that hurts.

AnikaSo what number do I use?

RevathiThe bad month. Call it $4,700. Budget to that and your good months turn into savings without any heroics. A budget is a forecast, not a prison. Forecasts need error bars.

AnikaYou're about to say "eightieth percentile."

RevathiI was going to say that.

AnikaOut of three months? That's not a percentile. That's three vibes.

Revathi(the pencil stops) Three data points are a rumor, not a distribution. You're right. Call it a guess with good manners. Use $4,700 until you have twelve months, and I'd rather be wrong on the cautious side.

AnikaFine. But here's what the app taught me. That $4,700 month was June. Move-in. First month's rent, a mattress, a shower curtain I am still emotionally recovering from. The app tagged all of it "one-time."

RevathiAnd the next one-time expense?

AnikaFlights home for the holidays. My roommate's wedding in the spring. A dentist I've been avoiding for two years.

RevathiSo the one-time expense happens every year. Only the label changes.

AnikaThat's the part the app can't see. It treats "one-time" like an exception.

RevathiThat is a better point than the one I was making. Surprises aren't rare. Each individual surprise is.

The Napkin

Revathi tears a paper napkin in half and flattens it on the yellow table. Assumptions come first, and they are illustrative: swap in your own.

  • Take-home pay: $5,200 a month (after tax and a 4% 401(k) contribution), in two checks of about $2,600
  • Planned spending, bad-month version: $4,700
  • Left over: $5,200 − $4,700 = $500 a month into a buffer

RevathiNow, why does the buffer exist? Don't say "because it's smart." Give me a number.

Anika...I can't.

RevathiThen let's price it. Say you hit a $1,000 surprise in about half of all years: car, dentist, a flight you can't skip. Say you hit a $5,000 one in about one year in ten: a laptop, a bigger repair, a rough stretch. Rough numbers, all of them. With no buffer, those go on a credit card at about 24%.

Surprise Chance in a year Interest on a ~24% APR card Expected cost
$1,000, paid off over 6 months 50% ~$72 ~$36
$5,000, paid off over 12 months 10% ~$674 ~$67
Total ~$103 a year

RevathiAbout a hundred dollars a year in expected interest. A $1,000 buffer costs you roughly $70 a year in forgone return, if you assume stocks would have earned about 7% and a savings account pays less than 1%. On the small surprises, it's nearly a wash.

AnikaWait. Then why do it?

RevathiBecause insurance has a negative expected value by design. You don't buy it for the average year. You buy it for the ugly one: two surprises in a single month, or the market down 20 percent the week your car dies, so you're selling investments at the bottom. The buffer isn't for the middle of the distribution. It's for the left tail.

AnikaSo my first investment is a savings account.

RevathiBoring is a position. You're buying the right not to panic.

The Disagreement

Anika's phone buzzes. She holds it up. It's Emma, with a chart that runs from the bottom left to the top right: this one is going to 5x by spring, I'm in, don't be dumb.

AnikaOkay, hear me out. $500 in that, versus $500 in a savings account, is obviously the better bet. Emma has literally never been wrong.

RevathiIs it a lock, or is it a story that ends with the word "5x"?

AnikaBe so for real, what are the odds?

RevathiYou tell me. What would you have to believe for it to 5x?

Anika...That Emma's right?

RevathiThat's the most honest answer anyone has given about a stock this year. I'm not forbidding it. I'm sequencing it. Buffer first. Your 401(k) match second. Then a small, capped account to learn on, and I'll help you build it. That is later.

AnikaAnd you? You keep forty thousand dollars in cash. I've seen the statement. It's been sitting there for ten years.

Revathi(turning back to the stove) That is a different chapter.

AnikaIt's going to be a very good chapter.

RevathiI am aware.

Monday Move (25 minutes)

  1. Find your bad month. Pull the last three months of spending from your bank app and take the highest. That is your budget number for now. Revisit it when you have twelve months. Ask which of those "one-time" expenses is a kind that happens every year.
  2. Open a separate savings account at a different bank from your checking. The small friction of logging in somewhere else is the point. Check today's rate rather than trusting last year's.
  3. Automate $500 the day after your first payday each month. If your employer allows split direct deposit, send it straight there so you never see it. Name the account something that keeps you honest, like "Future Anika's Car Repair."
  4. Look up your 401(k) match. Find out whether your employer matches, and up to what percentage. If you aren't contributing enough to get all of it, write that down as next week's job.

First target: $1,000. That covers most ordinary surprises. How big the whole fund should be is Chapter 2.

The Ledger

Anika opens the Ledger, a notes app on her phone where every entry gets a date stamp, a prediction and a percentage. She types her first three entries before she knows any of the answers.

# Prediction Probability Resolves
1 I have at least $1,000 in the buffer 85% Dec 31, 2026
2 I am still at this job 70% Sept 27, 2027
3 I break the Emma's-stock rule and buy something impulsively 60% Dec 25, 2026

Revathi(reading over her shoulder) Number three. You're low.

AnikaWhat would you put?

RevathiSeventy-five. Add mine too. If I'm right, I get to say I told you so on Christmas. If I'm wrong, it goes in the Kill Journal.

AnikaThe what?

Revathi(tapping a black notebook on the shelf above the stove) Paper. My worst calls, and what I believed at the time. September 2008: "The exposure is contained." It was not. Eat.

Anika picks up her spoon. On the napkin between them, Revathi has written one line in pencil and turned it to face her: Survival number = ?

The asterisk
