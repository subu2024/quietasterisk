The Plan Was Never the Plan

The first time I noticed it, I was waiting for a delayed train, staring at a departure board that kept pushing the time forward in five-minute increments — the classic small humiliation of public transit — and the man next to me was doing something I found quietly strange. He was writing in a paper planner. Not a phone. A planner. With tabs. Color-coded, from what I could see, in a way that suggested genuine emotional investment.

It was a Tuesday on a hot June morning. Nothing about the scene was dramatic. But I kept looking at that planner and thinking: what does he know that I don't?

I've been turning over the same question for a while now, in different forms. Why does planning long-term feel, to so many people, like an act of mild self-deception? You make the plan. Life does something else. You revise the plan. Life does something else again. At some point the plan and the life stop resembling each other entirely, and you're left holding a document that describes a person you're no longer sure you were trying to become.

And yet, some people plan with apparent conviction. Spreadsheets, five-year maps, vision boards, retirement calculators — whole industries exist to service the belief that the future is something you negotiate with in advance. These aren't stupid people. They're not naive. They've watched plans fall apart too. They just seem to have arrived at a different understanding of what a plan is for.

The departure board finally committed to a time. I put my phone away. The man with the planner clicked his pen closed, satisfied, as if something had been resolved.

My mother planned nothing. This was not chaos — it was philosophy. She grew up in a context where circumstances rearranged themselves without consulting her, and she had concluded, reasonably, that the energy spent mapping the future was energy stolen from managing the present. She was one of the most capable people I've known. She could improvise a meal, a solution, a conversation, from almost nothing. Planning, to her, was a kind of arrogance. The universe, in her experience, did not reward it.

My college roommate planned everything, including, eventually, his girlfriend. Not her — but the relationship's trajectory. There was a shared notes document between them, which he showed me once with the mild pride of someone who'd solved a problem others hadn't thought to solve. It had estimated timelines. Moving in together, somewhere between month eight and fourteen. The conversation about kids, no later than year three. It was color-coded. She had edit access.

He called any decision made without this kind of scaffolding "flying blind." I remember nodding when he said it, because it sounded responsible. It was only later I wondered who, exactly, the document was protecting him from.

Same uncertainty. Same universe. Two completely different stances toward it, both internally coherent, both inherited rather than chosen.

I've been thinking about that gap — not to adjudicate it, but because I'm not sure either of them was actually responding to uncertainty. I think they were each responding to something older, something that came before the question of whether to plan at all.

There's a physics problem tucked inside this that I find hard to stop thinking about. The planets are predictable. Eclipse dates are calculated centuries in advance. The solar system runs, more or less, like a mechanism — you can set it forward or backward and it behaves. This is not a metaphor. It is genuinely true that if you know the positions and velocities of the bodies involved, you can extrapolate almost indefinitely.

And yet we — who are made of the same matter, following the same physical laws — cannot reliably predict our own lives past the next several months. Sometimes past the next several days.

The standard explanations are real: complexity, feedback loops, the sheer number of variables in a human life interacting with other human lives. Chaos theory tells you that sensitivity to initial conditions means small differences compound into wildly different outcomes. This is true. But I'm not sure it's the whole story.

Because there's another layer. When you know a prediction about your own life, you can respond to it. Planets don't read their own forecasts. They don't decide, upon learning they'll pass within a certain distance of Jupiter, to adjust their trajectory out of stubbornness or hope or fear. We do. We are systems that incorporate information about the system into the system's behavior. This is either what makes us free or what makes us impossible to model, depending on the day.

There's a coffee shop I go to sometimes — the kind that is neither trying to be cozy nor aggressively minimal, just a room with tables — and I've started noticing the different ways people sit with their phones.

Some people are clearly executing a plan. They arrive, they work, they leave. The coffee is fuel. The time is accounted for.

Some people are clearly not. They look at their phones, then out the window, then at other people, then at their phones again. They ordered something complicated and seem to have already half-forgotten they're there.

I used to read the first group as disciplined and the second as distracted. I'm less certain now. The disciplined ones might be anxious — filling time to avoid sitting inside uncertainty. The distracted ones might be paying attention to something slower and harder to name.

Or neither. Or both.

The problem with watching people is that you're also watching yourself watch them, and what you see tends to reflect the questions you're already carrying.

Probability does something quietly devastating to the way we think about our lives. It can tell you — with genuine mathematical rigor — that there is a 70% chance of a given outcome. What it cannot tell you is which 70% you're in. You still have to live the actual outcome, not the distribution. The map describes the territory; it doesn't walk it for you.

And yet probability is also the only honest language we have for the future. Everything else is either false certainty or a refusal to engage. So we use it, knowing it's incomplete, the way you'd use a flashlight in a large dark room — not because it shows you everything, but because the alternative is standing still.

My mother would have found this mildly absurd. A flashlight you can't point anywhere specific. She would have preferred to move carefully and trust her feet.

My roommate would have wanted a better flashlight.

I keep thinking about the man on the platform with his color-coded planner. I don't know if whatever he wrote in that planner came to pass or dissolved, the way most plans do, into a different shape than intended.

What I keep coming back to is how settled he looked. Not because the future was resolved, but as if the act of writing it down had done something for him that was separate from whether it was accurate. Like he wasn't predicting the future so much as having a conversation with it.

Which might mean the plan was never really about the future at all.

There's a version of this where I land somewhere clean — where I tell you that planning is really about values, or identity, or something ennobling. And maybe it is.

But I've also noticed that the people most certain about how to handle uncertainty tend to be the ones who've been least surprised by it. And I'm not sure if that's because their method worked, or because the certainty itself is a kind of protection — a way of not fully admitting how little any of us are navigating by a map that was made for this specific terrain.

The departure board eventually showed a real time. The train came.

I got on. I didn't know exactly where I was going after I arrived. That felt, for a moment, like a problem.

Then it felt like something else.

I haven't quite decided what.
