> A question goes down the hallway
> and comes back wearing someone else's coat.
> Nobody remembers who let it out.
> The light on the machine is still orange.

The Keurig on the fifth floor has been asking to be descaled since April. There is a small orange light and a single word, DESCALE, blinking with the patience of something that knows it will outlast you .. The coffee still comes out, technically. It arrives in a thin, reluctant stream, about six ounces of hot brown water that vaguely remembers a Dunkin' French Vanilla pod, and the machine makes a sound like a man clearing his throat before delivering bad news.

Someone stuck a Post-it on it in May. *Facilities has been notified.* The note has curled at the corners and gone the color of weak tea. Nobody has taken it down, because removing it would be a kind of claim, and nobody wants to be the person who owns the Keurig.

I stand there most mornings around 8:15. The thermostat holds the floor at 68 degrees, which I know because a colleague once taped a small thermometer to a pillar as a form of protest that nobody acknowledged. I keep a gray fleece on the back of my chair year-round. In August it's a costume, and in January it's a necessity. At the machine my stomach makes a noise loud enough that Ash from policy glances over, the body announcing that breakfast was a rumor. I press the button and wait. I count the drips, which I never decided to do, but I've done it long enough to know a cup takes forty-one seconds.

There are usually two or three of us in the kitchen, and none of us mentions the light. We say *morning* and *how was the weekend* and study the pod drawer with real interest. It's a truce, and I think it may be the healthiest relationship on the floor.

And, here is what was waiting on my screen when I got back to my desk.

On Tuesday, in a leadership sync on Microsoft Teams, someone at the top asked a question. I wasn't in the meeting, but I've heard it described, and it was five words long: *What's our recent hiring number?* It was a real question, asked the way you'd ask whether it rained overnight.

By the time it reached my director, it had become a request for "a perspective on talent acquisition pipeline." By the time it reached my manager, it was "leadership wants a deep dive." By the time it reached me, on Wednesday, in a Teams message with a small hourglass emoji, it was a twelve-month hiring trend analysis with a recommendations tab. Nobody lied. Each person just sanded the question a little, the way you'd sand a table, until it was smooth enough to hand along without a splinter ..

There was a kickoff call to align on scope, then a follow-up call to align on the alignment. Someone said *let's socialize this with the broader group.* Someone else said *I'll pre-wire the VP.* I ate a Chipotle bowl over my spreadsheet and found that the honest answer was *we're not sure, and there are three ways to count it, none of them official nor in sync.*

Then I did what I always do, which is turn *we're not sure* into *early indicators draft version.* It's a small alchemy, and I'm good at it. I once spent most of a Sunday making a single slide stand up straighter than the data underneath it. I'd like to say I was pressured into it, but nobody was there. I did it alone, on my couch, with the good lamp on.

My manager added a chart. Her director cut the caveat. Someone in the chief of staff's office asked us to *tighten this to one page.* Somewhere around the fourth layer, the thing we'd been asked was no longer the thing we were answering, and the thing we were answering was no longer quite true. But it was shaped like a deliverable, and that seemed to count.

By Friday the person who asked had moved on. There was a reorg, or an offsite, or possibly just a new priority with a better name. The deck went into a SharePoint folder called *Final_v7_REVISED* and stayed there, in the dark, with the others. Nobody did anything wrong. That's the part I keep turning over. It was thirty-odd people working in good faith, at real cost, to answer a question no one was still asking ..

I don't want to make this bigger than it is. Every office I've ever known has this same tide, and it comes in and goes out and leaves the same shells. What I can't stop noticing is how sincere it is. We each believe we're making the thing better. The sanding feels like care.

I think about the children's game, telephone, where the drift is the point and the laughing at the end is the reward. In an office nobody laughs. We call it alignment.

On Thursday afternoon, after the deck was sent, I went back to the machine. The light was still orange. Under the curled Post-it there was a second one now, in different handwriting, with a smaller, more hopeful pen. *Second request submitted.*

I pressed the button. Forty-one seconds .. I stood there counting them, and I thought about how many people a request like that has to pass through, and how much of itself it would lose on the way, and whether anyone would remember, at the end, that it had started as a light on a machine.

Then I took my six ounces back to my desk. It was warm enough, mostly. Behind me the light went on asking, and I went on being one of the people who had noticed.
