She's ten minutes late, and I'm doing the thing I always do when I'm early somewhere - turning a coaster over and over, reading the brewery's logo on one side and nothing on the other. It's a coffee shop with a bell over the door, and every time it rings I look up like a man checking whether the news is good or bad, and every time it's someone else's date.

My coffee’s gone lukewarm, and I'm doing math I've done before. Not the math of should I be here. The other math — the one where you try to figure out how long it will take, once she walks in, before you know .. _Is she the one?_

Because that's the thing nobody tells you about "knowing." Everyone acts like it's a fixed unit of time. Like a microwave setting. But I've clocked it at wildly different speeds over the course of one lopsided life, and I still couldn't tell you what the actual number is. I'm fifty-something years old and I am, as far as I can tell, still running the same broken stopwatch I had at twenty-four .. I just trust it now with the false confidence of a man who's used the same watch for thirty years and never once had it checked.

## 1993. The party

She walked in wearing black, said her name - Amrita - twice like she wasn't sure I'd caught it the first time, and some strange feeling triggered in me. I want to say it was her eyes, but honestly it might have been the fact that she said "Hi" like she meant it as a whole sentence. I didn't ask for her number. I didn't say much of anything, if I'm being accurate about it. I just stood there, twenty-four years old and freshly convinced that feeling something enormous entitled me to do absolutely nothing about it .. which is, I've since learned, its own kind of arrogance, mistaking silence for depth. At the time I filed this under "certainty." Ninety seconds and I was certain. I was so certain that I let her leave the party without a word, because what do you do with certainty that arrives with no instructions attached?

## 1997. The dining room table

Vidya sat across from me with her parents in the next room .. close enough to eavesdrop .. and I was newly landed from the United States, jet-lagged in that specific way where the ceiling fan feels like it's spinning at the wrong speed. She agreed with everything I said. Two hours of agreement. By the time she left, I remember thinking, this is what "right" looks like — a kind of frictionless yes, nodding all the way down.

Twenty-four hours later I was informed, secondhand, that I'd been dropped. Same two hours. Apparently they'd produced two entirely different verdicts, hers and mine, and only one of us had been told about it .. I want to say this taught me something. It mostly just taught me that two people can sit at the same table, eat the same overcooked samosas, and walk away having attended two different meetings.

## 1997. a few weeks on. A different table, a different pair of parents

Hema was short, sharp-cheeked, long hair, talked fast. Twenty minutes in, I'd said yes to marrying her — not out loud, not exactly, but in the way you say yes to a house you haven't fully walked through because the agent is standing right there and everyone's waiting on the porch. My own certainty-clock had apparently recalibrated itself without telling me. Ninety seconds in 1993. Twenty minutes in 1997. Nobody adjusts the thermostat and announces it .. It fell apart within days, over a rumor I never fully got the shape of. I remember being relieved, and I remember being disturbed by how relieved I was, because relief implies I'd known — somewhere underneath the twenty-minute yes — that it was wrong the whole time .. which raises the question of what the twenty minutes had actually been measuring. Apparently not her. Possibly just my own appetite for being wanted quickly.

## 1998. My own living room, a different mother and daughter

She came in dark-haired, bubbling, and sparkling with excitement. Yet there was no current, no microwave ding within me. I remember being almost offended by the absence of it, like I'd ordered spicy and gotten mild. What closed it wasn't a slow accumulation of small votes — it was my mother, who I was staying with that week, sitting me down and asking, plainly, whether I'd made up my mind. Then, a few days later, a call came from her side — the girl's family, wanting an answer. Not a suggestion this time. A question that wanted a yes or no before the call ended. I said yes before I'd fully worked out why, and only afterward did I notice I'd stopped waiting for the ninety-second feeling and started replaying, instead, how she laughed a beat late at the end of sentences, like she was double-checking the joke was actually funny before committing to it .. Not a verdict I reached on my own. A verdict extracted from me — half by my mother across the dinner table, half by a phone call from the girl's side — days after I'd have sworn I hadn't reached one yet.

We got married. It lasted eighteen years, which is either a very long time to be wrong or a very long time to be right and then stop being .. I've never settled on which, and eighteen years wasn't long enough to decide, apparently, though you'd think it would be. There was a house, then a house that felt like a waiting room, then a lawyer's office that smelled like carpet cleaner. She left. I remember standing in the driveway afterward doing the same math I'd done at the party in '93, except this time running it backward: not how long until I know, but how did I not.

## Today

Ding. The bell over the coffee shop door rings again. This time it's her .. tall, beautiful, elegant .. She sits, apologizes for the traffic, and I notice I'm doing the ninety-second check out of pure muscle memory, running the old software on new hardware, and getting nothing conclusive. No electric current. No frictionless yes. Just a woman ordering a flat white and mentioning, unprompted, that she flips coasters over too, reading the blank side just to have something to do with her hands, which she seems to find embarrassing and I find, for reasons I can't fully justify, disproportionately charming .. There it is, I think, and then immediately don't trust the thought, because I've had that exact interior sentence three separate decades in a row, attached to three entirely different outcomes, and a man with my track record has no business trusting his own weather report.

Here's the part I keep circling back to on the drive home: I used to think the verdict was the thing being measured — is this person right, yes or no — and that the clock was just incidental, a stopwatch running in the background while the real event happened. But somewhere between the ninety seconds and that call asking for an answer, I've started to suspect the clock was never incidental. The clock was the event. Ninety seconds is what certainty looks like when you're twenty-four and have never lost anything yet. Two hours is what it looks like when your whole family is in the room, watching you perform a decision for an audience. A question closed in a matter of days is what it looks like once you've stopped trusting your own timing altogether and let the people around you make the call for you .. Which would make this whole business less a question of who is right for me and more a kind of ongoing self-portrait, one blind date at a time .. a way of finding out, indirectly, how much time I currently think a person deserves before I decide anything about them. And that number, it turns out, has never once been about the person sitting across the table.

She's asking me something about the coffee. I should probably answer.
