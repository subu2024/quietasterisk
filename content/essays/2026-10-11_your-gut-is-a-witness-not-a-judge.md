> A decision is a bet placed from inside a fog,  
> by someone who mistakes the fog for the view.  

In the summer of this year (2026), I almost rented a townhome. The plan was tidy: rent the townhome, sell my condo, and start over somewhere new. I had the numbers, the timeline, and a clear sense of what I wanted.

Then something small happened that I couldn't put in a spreadsheet. The realtor handling the townhome was dismissive of me. I asked questions and got short answers. I raised a concern and it was waved off, as though I were a transaction to be processed rather than a person about to rearrange my life. Nothing he said was technically wrong. It was the tone, a quiet message that my questions were an inconvenience.

I backed out. The plan collapsed, I stayed in my condo, and for a while it felt all wrong. I had momentum and a plan, and I had walked away from both because of a feeling I couldn't fully explain. I half expected to regret it.

Then the market shifted and interest rates went sky high. The move I'd almost made would have been far more expensive than the one I stayed put for. In hindsight, the decision that felt wrong at the time looks like the best one I made all year.

Here's the part I've been sitting with. It would be very easy to tell this story as *I trusted my gut and my gut was right.* It's a satisfying version, and it's probably wrong.

Everything I think starts from where I'm standing. My values, beliefs, and old bruises are the lens, and I don't see the lens. I see the world through it and call that *the world*. The lens produces thoughts, the thoughts shape decisions, and the decisions meet a reality that doesn't consult me. When I backed out, I had no idea rates would move. Nobody I asked did either. The outcome I'm now so pleased with came from a part of the chain I never controlled.

Outcomes are probabilistic, which is not the same as random. A coin flip is random, and no amount of care changes it. Life is closer to a weighted coin. A good decision tilts the weight in your favor, but you only ever get one flip. So good decisions sometimes land badly, and bad ones sometimes land beautifully. Poker players have a name for confusing the two. Annie Duke calls it *resulting*, judging a choice by how it turned out. If you run a red light and nothing happens, you haven't proven the light was optional. You were just lucky, and luck is a terrible teacher because it always sounds so confident.

And resulting cuts both ways. If I'd gone through with the move and rates had dropped, I'd have called myself a genius. As it happened, I backed out and rates rose, so I got to feel wise. The same fog covered both versions, and I'd have felt equally certain in either one.

If I can't judge a decision by its outcome, what can I judge it by? As far as I can tell, the only honest answer is the process, and the first part of any process is watching the thing that's doing the deciding. I don't mean silencing it. When I can say *I'm uneasy, and I don't know why yet*, there's suddenly a small gap between me and the unease. The bias doesn't disappear in that gap, but it becomes visible, and you can't correct for something you can't see.

That gap is also where the old question lives. Do you decide with analysis or with your gut?

For a long time I thought this was a fight with a winner. The analytical people had the spreadsheets, and the intuitive people had the vibes and a story about wise instincts. Neither camp is stupid. They're each right about different kinds of decisions.

Some questions can be answered by analysis. Which loan costs less over ten years? Which route is faster? Those have answers, and your gut has no business overruling arithmetic.

Others can't be settled that way, no matter how long the spreadsheet gets. Who to trust, what career feels right, who to marry. These questions turn on values, and values aren't a column you can sum. Analysis can tell you what each path costs, but it can't tell you what you're willing to pay. Neuroscientist Antonio Damasio described patients with damage to the part of the brain that ties emotion to choice. They could reason about options fluently and still couldn't pick between them. A feeling, it turns out, is part of how a decision gets finished.

So I no longer ask which one to trust. I ask two smaller questions first.

*Can I undo this?* If the decision is reversible and the stakes are low, such as a restaurant, a trial subscription, or a side project, then speed beats perfection. Jeff Bezos reportedly sorts decisions into one-way and two-way doors, and the useful part is the sorting. Most doors swing both ways, and we agonize over them as if they were bolted shut.

*Is this one-way?* Selling a home is. Walk through that door and the other side is a different life. That's when I slow down and do the research first, because the gut is a poor witness when it hasn't seen the evidence.

Here's the rule I've landed on, and I hold it loosely. Do the homework, then treat your gut as a signal to investigate rather than an order. If your analysis says yes but something in you flinches, don't override the flinch, and don't obey it either. Ask what it might know that the spreadsheet doesn't.

Looking back, my flinch had a specific shape. It wasn't about the townhome itself, which may have been perfectly fine. It was about being dismissed while preparing to make a one-way decision. If a person can't take my questions seriously before I've signed anything, I have little reason to expect better once I'm committed. Maybe that was a real signal. Maybe he was just having a bad day, and I let one rude afternoon steer a major decision. I can't tell, and I don't think I'm supposed to be able to.

If it goes the other way, and your gut says yes while the numbers say no, be suspicious of the gut. Desire is very good at hiring its own lawyers. The stronger the pull, the more carefully I check who's paying for the argument.

So did I make a good decision? I think so, but not because of how it turned out. I paused on a one-way door, listened to a doubt I could only partly explain, and chose to keep my options open when I couldn't see the whole picture. That would have been a decent process even if rates had fallen and I'd spent the next year wondering what I'd given up.

I still catch myself doing the other thing. Last week I called a perfectly reasonable choice "a mistake" because it rained on the day I'd planned around. Rain is not a verdict on my judgment. I noticed, a few seconds late as usual, and the noticing counted for something. Some days that's all the process gives me, and I'm trying to treat it as enough.
