> A life is not measured by what it held,   
> but by who would notice it gone.  

At the end of September, [a woman in Tennessee](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cmlyle97pqd7o) was strapped to a table to be executed. She was given a lethal injection, then a second one. She didn't die. A few days later, her lawyers said she was awake and speaking.

I'm not going to argue about her sentence here. What stayed with me is smaller. The state had a schedule, a protocol, a trained team and a backup dose. It still got wrong the one thing it was most sure of.

If the people who build whole systems around timing a death can't time it, I have no chance. I keep a calendar accurate to the half hour, and I have no idea which day holds the biggest entry of my life.

For a while I treated that as a planning problem. How do you plan a life when it ends at an unknown time? I don't think that's the useful question, because plans can bend. The question that actually got under my skin came from the other way death arrives, with no hospital and no audience. Sometimes a person dies quietly in an apartment, a house or a room, and nobody realizes it for days.

That image disturbs me, and I don't think it's only because of death. It's because of what it asks: *if nobody notices my absence, what does that say about my presence?*

Perhaps the deepest wound inside that question isn't the fear of dying alone. It's the fear of having lived without leaving a mark on anyone.

## What we count, and what we can't hold

We usually measure a life by what it produced: careers, money, degrees, possessions, recognition. We ask whether someone became successful. But when I imagine the final moment, none of those things can hold my hand. What remains is the network of people I made: the ones who knew me, depended on me, laughed with me, argued with me, loved me, or simply expected to hear from me tomorrow.

That last one stays with me. *Expected to hear from me tomorrow.* It's such an ordinary thing, and it might be the whole point.

## Who would cry?

This is why the question *Who would cry?* is more profound than it looks. It isn't really about tears. It's about connection.

Start with what the question isn't asking. It isn't asking how many people would show up. A crowd is easy to confuse with closeness, and the two are very different things. Public mourning for a celebrity can be enormous and loud, and a lot of it is performed grief for someone the mourners never actually met. Meanwhile a person can be missed intensely by exactly one other human being, and that single loss can empty out an entire life.

So the question is really about attachment. Who would feel the gap in an ordinary Tuesday? Who knows how you take your coffee, which of your stories you tell badly, what you sound like when you're pretending to be fine? Who would be the first to think *something's wrong, they would have answered by now*? Tears are only the visible proof that someone's life had a place carved out for yours.

I also want to be careful here, because this can be turned into a cruel scoreboard. Plenty of people die alone who were deeply loved. My aunt was extremely popular in her day, with lots of friends, and she still died a lonely death. Others are surrounded at the end by people who barely knew them. Distance, illness, immigration, grief and bad luck all take people out of reach of the ones who'd notice. Being unnoticed is often circumstance, not a verdict on your worth.

But it tells me something I can't un-learn. Popularity is a measure of how many people know you. Connection is a measure of how many people would be changed by losing you, and it doesn't scale the same way. My aunt had the first for years. What I wonder, and can't answer for her, is whether the second had quietly thinned out while the first still looked full.

## The unglamorous evidence

There's one thing I can't talk myself out of. The [Harvard Study of Adult Development](https://news.harvard.edu/gazette/story/2017/04/over-nearly-80-years-harvard-study-has-been-showing-how-to-live-a-healthy-and-happy-life/) has followed people since the 1930s, and its directors have kept arriving at the same unglamorous finding: the quality of our relationships is one of the strongest predictors of how we live and how well we age. Nobody was surprised by the conclusion. People were surprised by how little of their time they'd given it.

So I've started asking a smaller, more practical version of the question. Not *who would cry*, but *who would notice if I went quiet for three days?* And the harder follow-up: *Whose silence would I notice?*

The second one is the one I tend to fail. It's easy to want to be missed. It's harder to do the dull work of being the person who notices. (Note to self: a three-day silent retreat doesn't count.)

## What I postponed

In my own life, I kept putting off visiting my mom, because of the distance and my own financial circumstances. Then suddenly she was gone, and I made the trip for her funeral. The irony isn't lost on me. That trip could have been made while she was alive. I still carry that guilt with me.

I don't say this to be forgiven, and I don't think there's a tidy lesson in it. I just know that the postponing felt reasonable every single time, one small, defensible delay after another, until there was no one left to postpone it for.

I don't have a method, and I'm wary of anyone who does. The end is unscheduled, the odds are unreadable, and a woman in Tennessee just reminded everyone of that. All I can tell is that tomorrow is the one thing I'm always assuming, and the people in my life are the one thing I'm always postponing. I'm trying to notice the mismatch, a few seconds late as usual, and let that count for something.
