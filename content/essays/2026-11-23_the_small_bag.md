She asked me what my ideal Sunday looked like, and I answered like a man filling out a form.  

*No agenda,* I said. *No appointments. Coffee. Maybe staying in bed.*   

Maya raised an eyebrow. Then she raised the other one.

*Staying in bed,* she repeated. *Doing what, exactly?*

*Reading,* I said, with the straightest face I could manage.

She laughed, that sudden, unguarded laugh that makes strangers at the next table turn around. I realised I had been trying to earn it for three dates.

## Maya, Before the Coffee

I should explain how a man like me ended up across a table from a woman like her.

I am a computer professional edging toward retirement, which is a polite way of saying I have spent thirty years convincing machines to behave and almost none convincing people. I am divorced. I have two grown sons who tolerate my puns. I keep a blog called *Living with Uncertainty,* where I take small, ordinary events and ask them what they are trying to teach me. Mostly they ignore me.

Maya Moorthy is forty-five, Indian-born, American by choice, and the most talkative person I have ever met. She has two children, a demanding job, a mortgage nearly paid off and strong opinions about all three. She talks the way some people drive: fast, with sudden turns, and a total confidence that the road will be there.

*Listen, I have to tell you what happened yesterday,* she said, on our first date, before the coffee had even arrived. *I was at the grocery store, right? And I met this woman who knows Priya. You know Priya? Anyway, why is everything so expensive now? It's ridiculous!*

I did not know Priya. I did not need to. I simply sat there, a quiet man being carried downstream, and felt something I hadn't felt in a long time. Entertained.

## Coffee in the Bed

On that first afternoon I told her about the year after my divorce. Not the polished version. The real one: the takeout containers, the days I cried silently, the slow, clumsy education of learning to cook for a family of three.

I told her about my son, who once brought me coffee in bed. It was coffee, but not to my taste. Lukewarm, too sweet, somehow gritty. Yet I drank every drop.

Maya put down her cup and looked at me for a moment longer than people usually do.

*You,* she said, *are going to be a problem.*

*Why?*

*Because I was going to find you boring, and now I can't.*

I wrote down that sentence later, on a napkin, like evidence.

## The Runway

The second date was an early-morning coffee at a place overlooking an airport runway in Washington. I chose it because I thought planes taking off would be romantic. This is the kind of reasoning that has defined my life.

I was late. Eleven minutes. Maya was already there, arms folded, watching a jet climb into the grey as though she had a grievance against it.

*I'm sorry,* I began.

*Don't,* she said. *Sit. Drink something. I'm irritated and I'd like to be irritated for a minute, if that's all right.*

It was all right. I have learned, over the years, that people sometimes need their weather to pass before you can talk to them.

During our conversation, I asked her about her past. What came next surprised me. She told me she was tired of being introduced, mentally, as *the divorced one.* Of people leading with it, as if it were her job title. She was a professional. A mother. A friend. A woman who liked old Hindi songs and bargains and dressing up on a Saturday night for no reason at all.

*I am not a story that ended,* she said. *I'm a person in the middle of one.*

I listened the way I always listen, which is to say analytically, turning her words over for a structure. She noticed.

*You're doing it,* she said.

*Doing what?*

*Solving me.*

She was right, of course. Then I told her about my own list of imagined defects, the height, the voice, the quiet worry that no woman would look at me and feel what I hoped to be looked at with. I said it lightly. She did not take it lightly.

*Attraction isn't a checklist,* she said. *It's whether someone feels like home when they walk in.*

We talked about music. She hummed a line from an old film song, [*Waqt Ne Kiya Kya Haseen Sitam,*](https://youtu.be/3TxjJCEKYvE?si=gVPKfptXXP88_psL) and I felt my ears warm, because it is one of my favorites and I had never heard anyone hum it in daylight.

Then she said, gently, *I think you intellectualise your sadness, Sam. I think you write about it so you don't have to sit in it.*

The jet had gone. The runway was empty.

*That,* I said, *is annoyingly accurate.*

When we stood to leave, I asked, and moved closer. She did not stop me. The first kiss was brief, almost stolen. Then I held her face in my hands, and it became long and tender, and time stopped for a second. And I walked to my car with the specific lightness of a man who has been seen and not sent away.

## Three Weeks

Then nothing.

I will not pretend I handled it well. The first week, I told myself she was busy. The second, I told myself she was reconsidering. By the third, I had composed and deleted a dozen messages, each more reasonable and more pathetic than the last.

The old reflex arrived right on schedule: *Pull in. Get small. Protect the soft parts.*

But I had just spent a morning being told I hide inside my own thinking. So I did something that felt, for me, like skydiving.

I sent two words.

*All ok?*

The reply took most of an evening. When it came, it was a suggestion for coffee.

She apologised before she sat down. She told me she had written to me three times and sent nothing. That work had swallowed her, and the children, and the ongoing business with her former husband that she described only as *a lot of noise about nothing.* But underneath that, she said, was a simpler thing.

*I liked it too much,* she said. *The kiss, the Saturday, you. I started looking forward to seeing you, and it scared me. So I did what I do. I went quiet. I'm not proud of it.*

I told her the truth, that I had missed her, that the silence had been loud, and that I had wondered whether I was feeling infatuation or something with a longer shelf life.

*And?* she asked.

*I don't know,* I said. *I'm told that's the whole point of the blog.*

She smiled at that. Not the big laugh, the smaller one. The one I was beginning to think was reserved.

## Terms and Conditions

We had, by then, a third date. It lasted most of an afternoon, and it was the first time either of us stopped performing.

I told her I wanted to be part of her ordinary days, not only the good dresses and the good restaurants. She told me, firmly, that her ordinary days were not glamorous. Some days she was tired. Some days she wanted every living creature, including the people she loved, to leave her alone.

*I want company,* she said. *I don't want another responsibility.*

*That seems fair.*

*Does it? Most people hear that and sulk.*

I suggested we try to talk once a day. She frowned. I explained that I am a poor texter, that when I reply easily and naturally it means I am comfortable, and that if I go silent it is usually just me, being me, in my shell. We agreed there would be no rule. Just an effort.

Then, because I could feel the conversation loosening, I told her I was a comedian. For people who know me.

*Puns,* I admitted. *Mostly puns.*

*Oh no,* she said, delighted.

We argued about tattoos, which I dislike in quantity, and about loud lipstick, which I find difficult in close quarters, and about smoking, on which she had opinions of her own and no intention of surrendering them to me. I mentioned the small whisky I pour most evenings. I suggested that the right company might gently talk me into other uses of that hour.

She studied my face. *That wink,* she said, *is not as innocent as it thinks it is.*

*It's a very honest wink.*

*It's a suspicious wink.*

I saved the details, I told her, for a future conversation involving close proximity and lowered voices. She laughed so loudly the barista looked up.

## The Sunday Philosophy

That was when she asked about Sundays.

I described my ideal one, and she let me finish, which for Maya is a minor miracle. A lazy morning. No schedule. Coffee, bed, takeout or something interesting in a pan. Two people in no hurry.

Then I added what I always add. That it's an ideal, not a promise. That I have sons and responsibilities. That dreams and calendars compete for the same hours, and the calendar usually wins.

*Mine are laundry, groceries, a soccer game, a birthday party and a child who suddenly needs poster board,* she said. *But I like it. Maybe not the whole day. Twenty minutes on a sofa, no phones. That would do something to me.*

*Undivided attention,* I said. *That's my one condition.*

*Fine,* she said. *Except if one of my kids calls. Then I answer.*

*Of course.*

*Motherhood has privileges.*

*And if you're interrupted mid-kiss?*

She tilted her head. *Then I reserve the right to resume proceedings.*

I said I would accept that clause without amendment.

## Falling, Admitted

It is a strange thing to say aloud, at sixty-whatever-I-am, in a coffee shop, to a woman holding a paper cup.

*I think I'm falling for you,* I said. *You don't have to say anything. I just didn't want to hide it.*

Her eyes did the thing they do. Widened first, then narrowed, then softened.

*Sam,* she said. *I wasn't ready for that.*

*I know.*

*I think about you. I look forward to seeing you. I haven't forgotten that kiss. But I'm not going to say a word I'm not sure of. I want to be careful with something that might matter.*

Then she asked the question I had been quietly dreading.

*Are you falling for me? Or for the idea of someone?*

The honest answer took a moment to find.

*A little of both,* I said.

She nodded slowly, and I saw that I had passed something. Not a test. A kind of door.

*Me too, probably,* she said. *So here's my condition. Don't fall for a perfect version of me. I'm grumpy. I interrupt. I give advice nobody asked for. I'm the person who says she'll call you back and doesn't.*

*I'd like to meet her,* I said. *The real one.*

*She'll steal your blanket.*

*I have extras.*

## Mistaken Kiss

The afternoon ended the way good afternoons do, reluctantly.

She hugged me at the car and went for my cheek. I turned my head at exactly the wrong moment, or perhaps exactly the right one, and her kiss landed on my lips.

She pulled back, laughing, mortified, pleased.

*That was an accident,* she said.

*Entirely,* I said, as I pulled her closer and kissed her full on the mouth.

*This was no accident,* I said.

She did not pull away. She held on to the front of my jacket for a moment, as if checking that the ground was still there.

*Well,* she said, a little breathless. *I did reserve the right to resume proceedings.*

That evening, still holding the afternoon like a warm cup, I sent her a message I had been composing in my head for days.

*By the way, Maya means illusion. Hope you don't remain an illusion and one day become reality.*

The dots appeared and disappeared three times.

Then: *I don't want to be an illusion, Sam. I want to steal your blanket. Argue about restaurants. Forget to reply for six hours and drive you crazy. And maybe one day sit next to you and steal a sip of your whisky.*

I read it four times. A line that begins with an illusion and ends with a stolen sip of whisky is not something a man recovers from quickly.

## A Whole Day

The next morning, buoyed and slightly foolish, I proposed something.

*What if we had a whole day? No clock. Not coffee-and-a-quick-lunch. An entire day.*

She said yes before I finished the sentence. She liked the thought of conversation with no edges, of finding out who we were when nobody had to be home by four. We settled on Saturday the twenty-eighth, giving her time to arrange things with the kids.

Then, because honesty has become something of a habit, I clarified.

*When I say a whole day,* I wrote, *I mean the whole day. Coffee in the morning. The day. The night. Breakfast the next morning. I'd drive you home after.*

Three dots. Then nothing. Then three dots again.

*I like the idea of waking up together,* she finally wrote. *Breakfast without rushing. I really do. But the night is a different step for me, Sam. I'm not saying no. I just need to feel good about it when we get there.*

*I'm pushing my luck,* I admitted.

*You are,* she wrote. *It's kind of sweet.*

*We don't need to decide now,* she added. *Let's just have the day. Let the rest happen when it's right for both of us.*

## Friday Night

It is Friday evening as I write this, and the house is very quiet.

I have cleaned the kitchen twice. I have changed the sheets, then stood looking at them, wondering whether changing them had been presumptuous. I have put a spare toothbrush on the bathroom shelf, then moved it to the drawer, then moved it back to the shelf, an act of pure comedy that I would not wish on my worst enemy.

I have poured a small whisky. I am not drinking it yet.

Somewhere in Fairfax, a woman is probably telling her children to hurry up, looking for something she has put in an obvious place, and half-listening to three conversations at once. I imagine her in the middle of it, one eyebrow raised at the chaos, phone buzzing on the counter.

I keep thinking about what I wrote on my blog, years ago, before any of this. *That uncertainty is not a problem to solve but a weather to live in*. I believed it then. I believed it the way you believe things you have never been tested on.

Tomorrow I will pick her up. We will have coffee, probably very good coffee, and probably an argument about where to eat. We will walk somewhere, hold hands or not, say something clever or something foolish. The day will unfold the way days do.

After that I don't know.

My phone lights up on the counter.

*Still on for tomorrow?* Maya has written. *Wear something warm. And bring a bag. Small one. Don't read anything into it.*

I read the message once, then again, then a third time, slowly, the way I read the last line of a book I am not ready to finish.

*A small bag,* I type. *For what?*

The dots appear.

They disappear.

They appear again.
