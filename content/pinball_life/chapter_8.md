## When Things Feel Connected but Aren’t

There are days when everything seems to go wrong at once. Not in a dramatic way. No single event is catastrophic. It’s just a sequence of small things, stacked close enough together that they begin to feel intentional. It starts with waking up later than planned. Not alarm-missed late, just late enough to feel rushed. I knock my elbow on the doorframe while pulling on a jacket. The coffee tastes off—burnt, maybe, or weak. I spill a little on the counter and wipe it up too quickly, leaving a faint sticky patch I’ll notice later with annoyance.

Outside, the bus arrives early and leaves without me, or arrives late and makes me late. Either way, I stand there longer than I want to, watching traffic move in uneven bursts. Someone brushes past me without apologizing. Someone else sighs loudly, as if we’re all personally responsible for the delay. By the time I get where I’m going, my mood has shifted. I’m sharper than usual. Less patient. Small inconveniences feel pointed. Then an email arrives that’s shorter than expected. No greeting. No sign-off. Just the information, stripped of warmth.

*Of course,* I think. *That figures.*

This is the moment where my mind begins to tie things together. The late start. The bad coffee. The missed bus. The tone of the email. In my head, they form a chain. Evidence of a kind of day. The sort of day where nothing quite cooperates. I feel justified in my irritation. The pattern is clear. Except—it isn’t.

What’s actually happening is a series of independent events, each with its own causes, each indifferent to the others. The bus didn’t arrive late because I spilled coffee. The email wasn’t curt because I woke up rushed. The universe is not coordinating these things to test me. But it feels like it is.

I’ve always been susceptible to this feeling. The sense that once something goes wrong, the rest is likely to follow. As if events carry momentum, or moods invite consequences. I brace myself after the first inconvenience, expecting more. And because I’m braced, I notice them more readily when they arrive. This is how a day becomes “one of those days.” It took me a long time to realize how much of this is interpretation rather than fact.

**Independence**, in the way I’ve come to understand it, is the idea that some events have nothing to do with each other, even if they occur close together. They don’t conspire. They don’t compound by nature. They simply coexist. But the human mind struggles with this. We are meaning-makers. We look for connection instinctively. We assume proximity implies relationship. When two things happen back-to-back, we search for a link. Sometimes that link is real. Cause and effect exist. Patterns emerge for good reason. But often, we overreach.

I think about how easily I attribute intention to sequences. If one meeting goes badly, I walk into the next one guarded. If one conversation feels tense, I carry that tension forward, interpreting neutral interactions through its lens. The events themselves are independent. My reaction is not.

Once, years ago, I had a string of small mishaps in a single afternoon. I dropped my phone and cracked the screen. I missed a deadline by a few minutes. I received a piece of feedback that stung more than it should have. By the end of the day, I was convinced I was spiraling. That something fundamental was off. That I needed to “get it together.” Looking back, none of those things were connected. The phone slipped because my hands were full. The deadline was missed because I misread the time. The feedback was fair, if imperfectly delivered. But at the time, they blended into a single narrative: *I’m losing control.* That narrative stayed with me longer than any of the individual events. It shaped how I spoke to myself for days afterward. This is the quiet danger of assuming connection where there is none. We turn coincidence into character judgment.

I’ve noticed this especially in how I think about luck. If something good happens early in the day, I feel buoyed. I move more confidently, assume cooperation. When something bad happens early, I do the opposite. I tread carefully, as if the ground has become unstable. The early event becomes a kind of forecast. But the day does not know how it began. It does not remember. It does not adjust its behavior accordingly. Only I do.

There’s a superstition embedded in this thinking, even when we don’t recognize it as such. The belief that momentum exists independently of our actions. That good days and bad days are categories, not collections. I still fall into this trap. I still say things like, “It’s just one thing after another,” even when I know, intellectually, that it isn’t. What’s helped is slowing down enough to separate the pieces. *This happened. Now this is happening.* No arrow between them. No implication. This sounds simple. It’s not.

When you’re already frustrated, neutrality feels dismissive. You want validation. You want to acknowledge that the day feels stacked against you. And maybe it does feel that way. Feelings don’t require logical consistency to be real. But interpretation does. I’ve learned to ask myself, gently, whether I’m responding to what’s in front of me, or to a story that’s already taken shape. Sometimes I even list things, mentally or on paper. It helps to see them laid out:

* Missed bus \*
* Cold coffee \*
* Brief email \*

Separate lines. No paragraph.

When I do this, the emotional weight shifts. The day stops being an accusation and becomes a series of minor inconveniences. Annoying, yes. But not indicting. This matters because how we interpret sequences affects how we behave next. If I believe the day is “going badly,” I’m more likely to snap. To withdraw. To make choices that reflect that belief. If I treat each moment as its own thing, I recover faster. I reset more easily. This doesn’t mean pretending everything is fine. It means not letting one thing speak for another. I’ve noticed how often we do this to other people too.

Someone makes a mistake, and suddenly everything they do is viewed through that lens. Each action feels connected, reinforcing a judgment that may not be fair. We say things like, “That’s just how they are,” when what we mean is, “I’ve linked a few unrelated moments into a story I now trust.”

Independence asks us to resist that impulse. To allow people—and days—to be inconsistent without being incoherent. This is harder than it sounds. There’s comfort in patterns, even negative ones. At least they explain things. At least they tell us what to expect.

Letting go of false connections means accepting uncertainty again. It means acknowledging that the next moment is not obligated to resemble the last. I’ve been practicing this in small ways. When something goes wrong, I try to pause before predicting what comes next. I remind myself that the future hasn’t been consulted yet. Sometimes I even say it aloud, half-joking: “That was its own thing.” It helps more than I’d like to admit.

The other afternoon, I had one of those days. A meeting ran long. Lunch was unsatisfying. I felt off, distracted. Then something unexpected happened. A friend called. Not for any reason. Just to check in. We talked briefly. Nothing profound. But the conversation lifted me. If I’d been committed to the story of the day, I might have missed it. I might have interpreted the call through the lens of irritation, or brushed it off. Instead, I noticed how easily the tone shifted. How little it took. The earlier events hadn’t doomed the day. They’d just happened. This realization doesn’t make life smoother, exactly. It makes it more open.

When events are independent, then recovery is always possible. No moment has to carry the burden of the ones before it. There’s something hopeful in that.

I’m not always good at remembering it. Especially when I’m tired. Especially when I’m already frustrated. That’s when the mind wants to collapse complexity into a single verdict. But even then, a part of me knows better now. A quieter part that says: *This does not mean what you think it means.* That voice doesn’t erase the feeling, but it keeps it from hardening.

As I’ve grown more attentive to these mental shortcuts—the way I link events, assign momentum, build patterns too quickly—I’ve also started to notice another force at work in my life. Not the illusion of connection, but the reality of it. Because while many things are independent, not everything is. Sometimes paths really do cross. Sometimes one small, unplanned interaction genuinely alters what comes next. And learning to tell the difference between false patterns and real intersections—that feels like the next lesson waiting just ahead.
