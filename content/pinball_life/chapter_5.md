## The Story We Tell

There’s a house a few doors down from mine with an overgrown lawn. Not wildly overgrown. Not the kind that draws official notices or neighborhood gossip. Just enough that you notice it if you walk past regularly. The grass grows a little taller than the rest. The edges are soft. The weeds are winning, but politely.

I noticed it one spring morning while taking out the trash. The air still had that early-season chill, and the lawn stood out because everything else was just starting to wake up. Most people had already mowed. This one hadn’t.

At first, the observation was neutral. Just a fact. *That lawn hasn’t been cut yet.* But facts, I’ve learned, rarely stay that way for long.

A few days later, the grass was taller. Still no mower. No sign of activity. That’s when the story began to assemble itself, quietly, without my permission.

*Maybe they’re struggling,* I thought.

*Maybe they’re overwhelmed.*

*Maybe something’s going on.*

I pictured a person inside the house—tired, distracted, burdened by something invisible. Illness. Job stress. A relationship unraveling. The lawn became a symbol, standing in for a life slightly out of control. I didn’t realize I was doing this until much later. At the time, it felt like empathy.

I walked past that house almost every day. Each time, I added a small detail to the narrative. The curtains were drawn—clearly, privacy was important. The mailbox was full—busy, perhaps. The car hadn’t moved—working from home, or maybe unemployed.

The story grew richer, more specific. And because it was specific, it felt true.

This is one of the mind’s favorite tricks: the more detailed a story becomes, the more likely we think it is. A vague explanation feels flimsy. A layered one feels convincing. *They’re probably just busy* is boring. *They’re overwhelmed, dealing with something difficult, and letting small things slide* feels meaningful. Meaning, apparently, is persuasive.

There’s a name for this tendency—the **Conjunction Fallacy**, the belief that a complex, coherent story is more likely than a simple one, even when logic says otherwise. But long before I knew the term, I knew the feeling. The quiet satisfaction of having figured something out. The lawn, in my mind, was no longer just grass. It was evidence.

Weeks passed. The lawn continued to grow. So did my confidence in the story. I told myself I wasn’t judging. I was understanding. I was generous. I was reading between the lines.

One afternoon, I saw the neighbor for the first time. They were outside, kneeling near the driveway, adjusting something on their bike. They looked calm. Focused. Not distressed. Not hurried. Just someone fixing a bike.

This did not fit the story.

I felt a small flicker of discomfort. Not enough to abandon the narrative entirely, but enough to revise it. *Maybe they’re putting on a brave face,* I thought. *You never really know what people are dealing with.*

The story adapted. It always does.

A few days later, the lawn was mowed. Clean edges. Even lines. Completely unremarkable. The mystery ended without a reveal. I remember feeling faintly disappointed. Not because I wished the neighbor harm, but because the story dissolved so quietly. No explanation. No resolution. Just grass, cut late. That’s when I realized how invested I’d become in a narrative that had never been necessary.

I do this more often than I’d like to admit. Someone doesn’t reply right away, and I imagine a sequence of events that explains it. Someone seems distant, and I build a whole emotional backstory. A meeting goes awkwardly, and I decide it’s because of a deeper tension I’ve uncovered.

These stories feel helpful. They make the world feel legible. They reduce ambiguity. But they also crowd out simpler explanations. The neighbor might have been traveling. They might not care much about lawns. Their mower might have been broken. All of these explanations are not only possible—they’re common. They happen all the time. But they’re unsatisfying. They don’t make for a good story.

I’ve noticed this tendency especially when I’m uncertain or uncomfortable. The less information I have, the more eager I am to fill in the gaps. Silence invites interpretation. Ambiguity invites narrative.

Once, at work, a colleague seemed abrupt in a meeting. Short answers. Minimal eye contact. I spent the rest of the day replaying the interaction, constructing explanations. Were they upset with me? Had I said something wrong? Was there underlying conflict? Later, I learned they’d been dealing with a family emergency that morning. The meeting had nothing to do with me. My story collapsed instantly, replaced by a simpler, more accurate one. But until that moment, I’d been convinced of the more elaborate version.

There’s something humbling about realizing how often we prefer complexity over likelihood. How easily we mistake coherence for truth. The irony is that we often pride ourselves on being “good readers of people.” We trust our interpretations, especially when they feel emotionally nuanced. But nuance doesn’t guarantee accuracy. In fact, it can obscure it.

I think part of this comes from how we learn to understand the world. Stories are how we make sense of things. They have characters, motives, arcs. They explain why things happen. They’re how we teach children and entertain ourselves and remember the past. So, when something puzzles us, we instinctively reach for a story-shaped explanation. The trouble begins when we forget that not everything is a story.

Sometimes it’s just a lawn.

Sometimes it’s just a delay.

Sometimes two things are true independently, not because one caused the other or because they belong together in a larger narrative.

I see this most clearly when I look back at my own history. How many times have I interpreted a series of unrelated events as evidence of a pattern? A bad conversation followed by a bad mood followed by a bad decision, all woven together into a story about decline or failure. At the time, the story felt airtight. In hindsight, it feels forced.

We are very good at connecting dots. We are less good at asking whether the dots need to be connected at all.

I’ve noticed how this habit affects how we judge others, too. A person is not just late; they’re late and inconsiderate and unreliable. A coworker is not just disorganized; they’re disorganized and careless and unprofessional. Each added detail feels like it strengthens the case. In reality, it narrows our vision. There’s a quiet cruelty in this, even when it’s unintentional. We reduce people to stories that make sense to us, rather than letting them remain complex and sometimes contradictory.

I’ve been on the receiving end of this too. I can feel it when others decide who I am based on a handful of moments—projecting intentions onto me before I have time to clarify, reading my mind through a fixed lens, completely neglecting the nuance of what actually happened.

It’s not malicious. It’s efficient. But efficiency isn’t always kind.

One of the hardest things I’ve learned is to tolerate not knowing. To let a situation remain unresolved without rushing to explain it. This goes against every instinct I have.

When something doesn’t make sense, my mind wants closure. It wants a reason. It wants a story with a beginning, middle, and end. Letting go of that urge feels like leaving a sentence unfinished.

But there’s freedom in it, too.

When I notice myself building a narrative, I try now to pause and ask a simple question: *What’s the simplest explanation that would still make sense?* Often, the answer is dull. But often, it’s also accurate.

This doesn’t mean abandoning empathy or curiosity. It means holding them lightly. It means recognizing that our stories say as much about us as they do about the world.

The lawn taught me that. Not dramatically. Just quietly. Now, when I walk past houses with uncut grass, I still notice. I still wonder. But I try not to conclude. I remind myself that most things have ordinary explanations, and that ordinary doesn’t mean careless or broken.

It means human.

I still tell stories. I don’t think that will ever stop. But I’m learning to tell them with a question mark instead of a period.

*This might be what’s happening,* I think.

*Or it might not.*

That small shift matters. Because once we believe a story completely, we stop seeing alternatives. We stop gathering information. We stop being surprised. And surprise, I’m realizing, is not something to avoid. It’s often the first sign that we’re paying attention again.

As I’ve become more aware of the stories I tell, I’ve also started noticing another pattern—how often I confuse meaningful sequences with meaningful effort. How easily I assume that outcomes are tied neatly to intention, even when chance is doing most of the work. That realization has been unsettling in a different way. Because if stories aren’t always true, then control might not be either.
