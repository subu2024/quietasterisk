*Sunday, October 4, 2026. Revathi's kitchen, Fairfax, Virginia.*

The group chat at Anika's company has forty-one unread messages. The first is from Marcus in finance: *heard the Series B isn't closing. not saying anything. saying nothing.* The second is a gif of a man sweating. By lunch, three people have quietly updated their LinkedIn profiles.

Anika does not update hers. She opens her savings account instead. Thursday's transfer landed right on schedule: $500, labeled *Future Anika's Car Repair*. It looks very small.

At her mother's house she finds Revathi cutting carrots into identical coins, which Anika knows means she is thinking about something she hasn't said out loud.

**Anika:** Hypothetically. If a company is "restructuring"...

**Revathi:** Is it restructuring, or is there a rumor about restructuring?

**Anika:** A rumor. Marcus in finance says the Series B isn't closing. Three people updated their LinkedIn before lunch.

**Revathi:** And you?

**Anika:** I looked at my buffer. It says $500. It looks like a rounding error.

### The Question

**Anika:** How many months of expenses should I actually have saved? Everyone says three to six. Nobody says why.

### Revathi's Frame: you are sizing a chance, not a number of months

**Revathi:** Because "three to six months" is a slogan, and a slogan is the average of other people's lives. Let's price yours. There are two odds. One: how likely is it that you lose this job in the next year? Two: if you do, how long until the next paycheck? Multiply them and you get the chance you run out of money. That is the number the buffer exists to shrink.

**Anika:** So it isn't really "how many months." It's "how likely am I to run out."

**Revathi:** Both. Months are the tool. The chance of running out is the goal.

**Anika:** Where would I even get those two odds? I have no idea how likely I am to be laid off.

**Revathi:** Nobody does, to the decimal. You make a first guess, write it down, and let evidence move it. A rumor moves it up. A signed contract with a new customer moves it down. The mistake is not guessing badly. The mistake is refusing to guess because the number can't be perfect, and then defaulting to a slogan.

### The Napkin

Revathi wipes her hands, finds a pencil and a fresh napkin. Everything here is illustrative: replace each number with your own.

<div class="napkin" markdown="1">

- **Essentials: $3,200 a month.** Rent share $1,900, groceries $450, car and transit $380, phone, health and renters insurance $190, loan minimums $280.
- **Chance you lose this job involuntarily in the next year:** 15% (rough, and nudged up by the rumor).
- **How long a search takes if you do:** 60% of people find work within 3 months, 85% within 6, 95% within 9 (rough).
- **Cost of holding cash:** savings pays about 4% and invested money might earn about 7%, so each dollar in the buffer costs about 3 cents a year in forgone return [CHECK CURRENT: savings rate].

Survival number = monthly essentials × months.

</div>

<div class="tw" markdown="1">

| Buffer | Survival number | Chance a search outlasts it | Chance you run out in a year (15% × that) | Yearly cost of holding it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 months | $9,600 | 40% | 6.0% | ~$288 |
| 6 months | $19,200 | 15% | ~2.3% | ~$576 |
| 9 months | $28,800 | 5% | 0.75% | ~$864 |

</div>

**Revathi:** Each extra three months costs you about $288 a year. Going from three months to six cuts your chance of running out from 6% to about 2%. Going from six to nine cuts it from about 2% to under 1%. Same price, smaller prize. So the question isn't "what's the right number." It's "where does the next $288 stop being worth it to you?"

**Anika:** Okay, but this table assumes I earn nothing the second I'm laid off. That's not how it works for people my age. Three friends got let go last spring. All three were doing contract ops work within two weeks.

**Revathi:** Earning what?

**Anika:** About a third of their salary. Call it $1,200 a month by the end of the first month. Not great. But not zero.

**Revathi:** Then in those scenarios your real drain is about $2,000 a month, not $3,200, and my table overstates what you need. (She writes something on the napkin and crosses it out.) I would count that income only after you have done it once. A skill you've proven is an asset. A skill you've imagined is a story.

**Anika:** Fair.

**Revathi:** It also means "three to six months" was calibrated to a world where nobody had a bridge job in two weeks. I'll be updating my slogan.

### The Disagreement

**Anika:** I want three months. $9,600. That's nineteen months of what I'm saving now. And honestly, if everything went wrong, I could move home.

**Revathi:** I will always have a room for you. But a backstop you are counting on is not a backstop. It's a plan, and plans get priced. What if I'm traveling? What if the house is a mess? I'm your plan B, Annie. I'm not your risk model.

**Anika:** Okay. How many months is your forty thousand?

**Revathi:** (a long pause) I never sized it. It's a feeling that I've kept for ten years, not a number.

**Anika:** You never did the math.

**Revathi:** Don't make me regret saying that out loud.

They settle it in stages. First $1,000, which she'll hit on November 1. Then three months, $9,600. Revathi wants six, and Anika agrees to revisit it in January, when the rumor has either come true or gone away. At her average pace of about $800 a month (the $500 plus roughly $300 of slack, since she spends $4,400 on average against a $4,700 budget), she would need $860 a month from December to reach $9,600 by this time next year. That is $60 above her average.

### Monday Move (25 minutes)

<div class="move" markdown="1">

1. **Write down your essentials:** rent, groceries, transport, insurance and phone, minimum debt payments. Use last month's statements, not your memory. Add them up.
2. **Multiply by 3, 6 and 9.** Those are your survival numbers.
3. **Write your two odds:** your chance of losing your job in a year, and how long a search takes in your field. Ask two people in your field for the second one.
4. **Pick a stage target and a date.** Divide what's left to save by the monthly amount you can really send. If the date feels too far away, that's useful information.

*If you already have bridge income you've actually earned, note it separately. Don't count it until you've done it once.*

</div>

**Revathi:** Good. Write your odds now, before you know anything. That's the only time they're worth recording.
