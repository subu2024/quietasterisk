*Sunday, October 25, 2026. Fairfax, Virginia.*

Thursday, 11:52 p.m. An email from Parkline Apartments lights up Anika's lock screen: *Your renewal offer is ready. Subject to credit and rental screening.*

She screenshots it to the group chat with one line: "do I even HAVE a credit??"

Her roommate, passing in a towel, says, "Mine's fine, I think." Neither of them knows what that means.

On Sunday the onions go gold in the pan, and Anika sets her phone face-up on the yellow table, screenshot showing.

### The Question

**Anika:** Parkline wants to run a "credit screening." Why does my landlord need a grade on me? I pay on time. I've never been late for anything.

**Revathi:** Except Tuesday's standup.

**Anika:** Mom.

**Revathi:** Continue.

**Anika:** What even is a credit score? And ok but what do I do Monday?

### Revathi's Frame

**Revathi:** It isn't a grade, and it isn't a verdict on your character. Think weather. A forecaster can't tell you whether it rains on your street tomorrow. She says seventy percent, because of what happened on past days that looked like this one.

**Revathi:** A lender can't know whether you'll pay. It looks at the days that looked like yours.

**Anika:** So the score is the chance of rain.

**Revathi:** Roughly, squeezed into one number and then priced. A landlord reads it and sets your deposit. A lender reads it and decides how much umbrella to charge you. That charge is the interest rate.

**Anika:** And what moves the number?

**Revathi:** Call it five things, roughly. Two do most of the work: whether you pay on time, and how much of your available credit you're using when someone looks. Then the age of your file, new applications, and the mix of accounts.

**Anika:** So you can't build a score without borrowing.

**Revathi:** You build it by being seen paying money back. Say it in odds. Before you open that app: I'd call your score 700 to 740, eighty percent confident.

**Anika:** Be so for real. You're guessing.

**Revathi:** I'm estimating. Open it.

Anika thumbs the screen. "Six eighty-one."

Revathi's pencil stops over the napkin.

**Anika:** I'm twenty-three. My file is about three years old. Student loans and one starter card, $1,500 limit, used twice. You were picturing your file. Decades of it.

**Revathi:** I reasoned from the wrong reference class. Yes.

She takes the Kill Journal, her paper notebook of bad calls, off the shelf by the spice jars, writes one line, and puts it back. Anika pretends not to watch.

### The Napkin

**Revathi:** Let's price the gap. Assumptions first. All of these are illustrative. Real offers vary by lender, car, down payment and the day.

- A $20,000 car loan, 60 months, same car, same price.
- Three rough bands with made-up cutoffs and made-up rates: strong (about 760 and up) at 6%, middle (about 680) at 10%, weak (about 620) at 15%. 

| Band (illustrative cutoffs) | APR (illustrative) | Monthly payment | Total interest | Extra vs. strong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strong, ~760+ | 6% | $387 | $3,200 | none |
| Middle, ~680 | 10% | $425 | $5,500 | +$2,300 |
| Weak, ~620 | 15% | $476 | $8,550 | +$5,350 |

**Revathi:** You're the middle row. About $38 a month, about $2,300 over the loan, for being the middle.

**Anika:** Translate it to rent. Or paychecks.

**Revathi:** Weak versus strong is $5,350. More than a month of your take-home.

**Anika:** Okay, but nobody takes sixty months.

**Revathi:** The textbook says sixty.

**Anika:** The textbook isn't at the dealership. They ask what monthly payment you want, then stretch the loan until it fits. Seventy-two, eighty-four. My coworker's car is eighty-four.

Revathi re-runs it, pencil scratching. At 72 months the strong band's interest is about $3,860. The weak band's is about $10,450.

**Revathi:** The gap grows to roughly $6,600. The monthly payment hides it, so the long loan hides the price. I modeled the version in the book. You modeled the version people live in.

**Anika:** I'm putting that in the notes app.

**Revathi:** Of course you are.

### The Disagreement

**Anika:** Here's my problem. All of this says "get a credit card." Cards are a trap. A girl from my floor junior year had $3,100 on one at, what, 28%?

**Revathi:** Call that figure rough. Say it's 28%.

**Anika:** Then that's about $72 a month just to stand still. Illustrative. And the app pings you: "You've earned a reward!" Reward for what? For spending.

**Revathi:** A card is a tool with variance. Same card, two outcomes. Paid in full, it's a free short-term loan and a record of your reliability. Carried at 28%, it's a leak. The variance lives in the person, not the plastic. So you don't ban the tool. You give it rules.

**Anika:** Which rules?

**Revathi:** For you, I'd pick one card. One small recurring bill on it, something you'd pay anyway. Autopay set to the full statement balance. An alert when the balance crosses 30% of the limit. 

**Anika:** Okay, that actually makes sense, I hate it. But your rules assume the version of me who reads emails. The app is built so I tap first and think later.

**Revathi:** Fair. Then the rules go into the machine. Autopay on. Push notifications off. The card out of your phone's wallet, so the tap isn't one tap.

**Anika:** That's a good one.

**Revathi:** It's yours. You just said it.

### Monday Move

Anika types her Monday into the notes app while Revathi stirs. Twenty-five minutes, her estimate.

1. Find the official source for free credit reports and pull every report it offers. [CHECK CURRENT: official site and how often reports can be pulled.]
2. Read each report for four things: name and addresses, every account listed, any account she doesn't recognize, and any late-payment marks.
3. Log in to each account with a minimum payment (the loan servicer, the starter card) and turn on autopay for at least the minimum.
4. Write down today's date and the score her app shows, knowing a lender may see a different one.

**Anika:** Checking my own report doesn't count as a hard inquiry, right?

**Revathi:** I believe that's right. And the minimum is a floor. For your card, the plan is the full statement balance.

### The Ledger

**Anika:** Entries. Written October 11, 2026.

1. My Monday report shows at least one item I don't recognize or that looks wrong, resolved Monday night, October 12, 2026. Me: 20%. Mom: 35%.

**Revathi:** Soft thirty-five. I have no base rate for it, so treat my number as a guess.

2. My app score is 700 or higher on January 31, 2027, with autopay on and one small bill on a card. Me: 50%. Mom: 40%.

**Revathi:** I don't know how fast a thin file moves. Call it forty and call it soft.

**Anika:** And I'm scoring you. You said 700 to 740, eighty percent. Actual: 681. A miss.

**Revathi:** A miss. Process review: a range I call eighty percent should miss about one time in five, so one miss doesn't indict the habit. What it does show is the input. I used my file instead of yours. Right method, wrong reference class.

**Anika:** So the outcome showed the hole in the process.

**Revathi:** Yes. Eat.

Anika's phone buzzes on the table. Emma, with a screenshot: *PRE-APPROVED: $20,000 auto loan. APR 22%.* Underneath, Emma has typed: "free money basically!!"

Revathi sets down the spoon. For a moment the only sound is the onions.
